\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Illegal dumping is a persistent municipal problem with an obvious
computer-vision framing, and the field has adopted that framing consistently.
Researchers collect clips, label each one as containing a dumping event or not,
train a classifier and report clip-level accuracy. The 2026 IWDD contest
\citep{bouwmans2026overview} gave this protocol a shared benchmark. Ten teams
trained on Mivia-IWDD (400 surveillance videos, balanced, with onset timestamps)
and were ranked on a private test set of 100 videos. Seven entries are published
\citep{almorsy2026x3d,kim2026kfold,scarrica2026garberus,kairanbay2026vlm,zbuce2026novelty,bhat2026imslab,putzu2026unicass}.
Each reports per-video precision, recall and $F_1$, together with a notification
delay. Where an entry counts false alarms, it counts them per clip, as in ``six
false alarms on thirty negative videos'' \citep{almorsy2026x3d}. That is
specificity, not a rate over a monitoring period, and the organisers' ROC
analysis is per clip too. The larger Mivia-IWDD-500 release \citep{greco2026iwdd},
with 250 positive and 250 negative videos, postdates the contest.

The framing does not match deployment. A camera watching a lay-by does not
receive a curated clip and return a verdict. It produces frames for weeks, and
the system alerts when its evidence first crosses a threshold. The municipality
manages the number of enforcement dispatches wasted over a season, not per-clip
precision. Testing a stream repeatedly and stopping at the first exceedance is
optional stopping. It invalidates the level at which the threshold was
calibrated.

The gap is larger than it looks. A rule that raises a false alarm on each frame
with probability $q$ raises at least one over $T$ frames with probability
$1-(1-q)^{T}$, which tends to one for any $q>0$. Persistence filters and score
smoothing change $q$, but not this limit. Feed a per-frame rule p-values that
are valid at the $1\%$ level, and at least one false alarm occurs on
\ValidityPfaPvaluePct\% of $40$-minute null streams (\cref{fig:validity}\,(a)).
A raw score threshold tuned to a per-frame rate of $10^{-3}$ reaches a
false-alarm probability of $0.980$ within one hour and $1.000$ from six hours
(\cref{fig:validity}\,(b)). A clip-level evaluation never runs the detector for
hours, so it cannot see this.

Nothing in this argument is specific to dumping. Any scene-level anomaly that a
per-frame backbone scores, such as loitering, wrong-way motion, an abandoned
object or a vehicle where pedestrians belong, faces the same gap. The method
therefore targets streaming visual anomaly surveillance, with illegal dumping as
the motivating case. Real-data validation covers UCSD Ped2 and CUHK Avenue, two
pedestrian-walkway corpora, and Mivia-IWDD-500, which contains illegal-dumping
footage. Each corpus is run with an optical-flow backbone and a PatchCore
backbone, and each test video is monitored alone. The Mivia labels give onset
times only, and the real streams are short. The long-horizon claims therefore
rest on simulation (\cref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Why the existing sequential machinery is the wrong shape}

The tools for optional stopping are mature. Ville's inequality \citep{ville1939}
bounds the probability that a non-negative supermartingale ever exceeds a level.
Game-theoretic work turns this into a construction: bet against the null, and
the accumulated wealth is a time-uniform certificate \citep{shafer2019game,ramdas2023savi}.
Conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005alrw} supplies distribution-free p-values to
bet on. E-detectors \citep{shin2024edetectors} adapt the machinery to change
detection, and e-BH \citep{wang2022ebh} combines evidence across streams under
arbitrary dependence. We know of no application of this machinery to waste,
dumping or pollution surveillance.

Importing it unchanged would not be enough, and the reason is structural. Every
one of these procedures models a persistent change. At an unknown frame $\nu$
the law switches from $P_0$ to $P_1$ and stays there. The mixtures run over
$\nu$ alone, and the optimality theory, thresholds and delay bounds all presume
this.

An illegal dumping event is not a persistent change. It is an \emph{episode}. A
van pulls up, the offender unloads for forty seconds and drives away. Within the
episode the signal is intermittent: the offender is occluded by the vehicle,
steps out of frame and returns. Then the episode ends and the stream reverts to
its null behaviour. A site used once is often used again, so another episode
follows weeks later.

Modelling that as a persistent change matters. A changepoint-mixture wealth
process keeps betting at full aggression after the episode ends, and it hands
back what the episode earned. It also counts the quiet frames inside an episode
against the alternative. Both effects lengthen delay, and the second grows with
intermittency, which is the regime this problem lives in. In paired simulations
on a shared grid, the episodic mixture reduces the mean censored delay by
141.1\,s (95\% CI +79.6 to +210.2\,s) for four recurring episodes of 1600 frames,
intermittency $\pi=0.2$ and a mean gap of 12{,}000 frames. A single episode
reverses the sign: the changepoint mixture is ahead by 55.7\,s (95\% CI 14.0 to
111.2\,s). Recurrence alone favours the episodic mixture, and \cref{sec:episodes}
maps the regimes.

\subsection{Contributions}

The paper makes three kinds of contribution: theory, an algorithm and an
evaluation method.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Theory: an episodic e-process.} Mixing over episodes rather than
      changepoints gives a wealth process that is an exact non-negative
      supermartingale, so Ville's inequality applies unchanged
      (\cref{thm:episodic}). It is a strict generalisation: it equals the
      changepoint mixture exactly when the episode-end rate is zero
      (\cref{prop:generalisation}).

\item \textbf{Theory: exact conformal levels.} Each attainable p-value level is
      replaced by its exact Beta order-statistic bound (\cref{thm:beta}). At
      $n=\CalN$ and $\delta=\CalDelta$, the smallest usable p-value falls from
      $\DkwPmin$ to $\BetaPmin$, an improvement of $\BetaOverDkwSpeedup\times$
      over the Dvoretzky--Kiefer--Wolfowitz bound.

\item \textbf{Algorithm: a constant-time recursion.} The mixture runs as a
      forward recursion with $O(1)$ time and memory per frame
      (\cref{eq:forward,alg:episodic}). Direct evaluation over active starts
      costs $O(t)$ per observation. The guarantee layer adds under a microsecond
      per frame (\cref{sec:compute}).

\item \textbf{A learned prior and a posterior, with a negative result.} A learned
      spatial prior enters only as a predictable modulation, so it cannot break
      the guarantee (\cref{thm:predictable}). The posterior probability of being
      inside an episode is bounded in $[0,1]$, which suits a message-passing
      layer across cameras. The spatial-prior architecture built on it did not
      shorten delay (\cref{sec:network}).

\item \textbf{An evaluation of guarantees.} We report realised false-alarm rates
      over up to \ExpOneReps{} streams per operating point, against six baselines
      including a reimplementation of E-SHIFT \citep{khan2026eshift}. Delay is
      reported censored as well as conditionally (\cref{sec:problem}). Real video
      is monitored per video, with operating points chosen on training videos
      only (\cref{sec:real-data}). Dilated betting did not help intermittency
      (\cref{sec:dilation}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.}
The changepoint mixture with retained tail mass is not new. It is the
PFA-controlled e-detector of \citet{saha2026nonpartitioned}, which gives
$\mathbb{P}(\tau<\infty)\le\alpha$ for a mixture over start times with weights
summing to one. Our contribution is the episode axis on top of it, and the
$O(1)$ recursion that makes that axis cheap. Conformal prediction and e-BH are
existing machinery, used as intended. \cref{sec:limitations} sets out what the
evaluation does and does not establish.
