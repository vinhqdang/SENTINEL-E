Real video tests whether the guarantee holds per video on footage that the
simulator does not produce. Three corpora supply it: CUHK Avenue
\citep{lu2013avenue}, UCSD Ped2 \citep{mahadevan2010ucsd} and Mivia-IWDD
\citep{greco2026iwdd}, an illegal-dumping corpus. Each corpus is run with two
training-free frozen backbones. Mivia-IWDD is also run with a supervised backbone
trained on its training videos. No backbone is fine-tuned.

\paragraph{Data and backbones.} Ped2 has \RealPedTwoNTrainVideos{} training
videos, all normal, and \RealPedTwoNTestVideos{} test videos from one fixed
camera. Avenue has \RealAvenueNTrainVideos{} training and
\RealAvenueNTestVideos{} test videos from one fixed camera. Mivia-IWDD has 400
training videos, 200 labelled negative and 200 positive, and 100 test videos: 50
negative, 25 static dumping and 25 dynamic dumping. The 200 negative training
videos are the calibration pool for every Mivia detector. The 200 positive
training videos are used only to train the supervised backbone. Each Mivia video
comes from a different camera, so Mivia calibration pools cameras. The labels
mark only the dumping onset. Mivia is sampled at 10\,fps; the other two corpora at
25\,fps.

The flow backbone is Farneback optical-flow magnitude \citep{farneback2003},
averaged per frame at $64\times64$ resolution and median-filtered over three
frames within each video. The appearance backbone is PatchCore
\citep{roth2022total} with a frozen WideResNet-50-2 pretrained on ImageNet.
It uses layers 2 and 3, a 1\% coreset of training patches, and the maximum
over patches of the nearest-neighbour distance as the frame score. Its frame
AUCs are \RThreePedTwoFlowAuc{} (Ped2 flow), \RThreePedTwoPCAuc{} (Ped2 PatchCore),
\RThreeAvenueFlowAuc{} (Avenue flow), \RThreeAvenuePCAuc{} (Avenue PatchCore),
\RThreeMiviaFlowAuc{} (Mivia flow) and \RThreeMiviaPCAuc{} (Mivia PatchCore).
The Mivia flow score is below chance. PatchCore is a strong training-free
appearance backbone, but it is not the state of the art on these benchmarks. Its
frame AUC is 0.828 on Ped2, 0.737 on Avenue (below flow's 0.783) and 0.588 on
Mivia. The power results below therefore bound what such frozen backbones
deliver, not what the best published video anomaly detectors would deliver. We
did not add a state-of-the-art video anomaly detector. Such models are trained per
dataset, and evaluating them without leakage would need its own training-only
selection protocol. Ped2 and Avenue training videos contain no labelled anomalies,
so the supervised backbone cannot be built for them under the same protocol.

The supervised backbone is a logistic regression on frozen features from a
Kinetics-400 pretrained MViT-B video transformer (torchvision \texttt{mvit\_v1\_b}). The architecture was fixed before any classifier was trained, as the strongest of three candidates (R(2+1)D-18, Swin3D-T, MViT-B) that fitted the available GPU time in a throughput benchmark; no candidate was scored on test videos.
For every frame at 10\,fps, the input is the causal clip of the last 16 sampled
frames ending at that frame. Features are standardised, and the regularisation
strength is chosen from the fixed grid $\{10^{-3},\dots,10\}$ by grouped five-fold cross-validation over the
400 training videos, stratified by class; the smallest grid value was selected, and the grid was not extended afterwards. Each training video is scored by the fold
model that did not see it, and each test video by one fold model. Frames at or
after the onset of a positive training video are labelled 1 and all other frames 0.
The score is the logit, smoothed by the same three-frame median as the other
backbones. Its frame AUC on the test videos is \RThreeMiviaSupAuc{}. Unlike the
other backbones it uses labelled dumping from other cameras, so its results answer
a different question: how much power a detector trained on the corpus reaches
under the same per-video protocol.

\paragraph{Protocol.} The unit of replication is the video. Each test video is
monitored alone by a freshly started detector, and no frame is reused between
calibration and monitoring. Calibration uses training videos only. For Ped2
and Avenue it uses all training videos of the same camera. For Mivia it uses
the normal training videos of the other folds, pooled across cameras. For the
supervised backbone these calibration scores are the cross-fitted scores described
above, so no calibration video is scored by a model trained on it.

Operating points are selected on training videos only, before any test video
is read. SENTINEL-E's betting lag $L^\ast(\alpha)$ is the smallest lag on the
grid $\{1,2,3,5,7,10,15,20,30,50\}$ whose leave-one-video-out (LOVO)
per-video false-alarm proportion is at most $\alpha$. Ped2 and Avenue use
LOVO over their 16 training videos. Mivia uses 10 folds by video index, each
holding out 20 normal training videos. Each classical baseline is thinned at the same
$L^\ast(\alpha)$. Its knob is the first admissible knob in sweep order, from the
most aggressive. A knob is admissible when its LOVO proportion is at most
$\alpha$. If no knob is admissible, the most conservative knob is used. This
happens for \RThreeBaselineNoKnob{} of the \RThreeBaselineConfigs{} baseline
configurations at $\alpha=0.1$, and those results describe a conservative
setting, not a tuned one.

This replaces an earlier evaluation in which the betting lag was tuned on test
streams and normal frames were reused across spliced streams.

A null trial is a video with no anomaly, or the pre-onset part of an anomalous
video that lasts at least 2\,s. On Mivia the null trials are the 50 negative
test videos. A detection is an alarm inside an episode window, extended by a
one-second grace period. The detector restarts after each alarm, so a video
can contribute more than one alarm to the rate per hour. It counts once in the
per-video proportion. All proportions use exact Clopper--Pearson intervals.
False alarms per hour use an exact Poisson interval. The effective sample size
is stated with each result: the number of null videos, and the number of
episodes. Avenue has 74 episodes in 21 videos, so its detection intervals treat
episodes as independent and are optimistic.

The first finding concerns validity. On Ped2 and Avenue the false-alarm counts at
$\alpha=0.1$ are small. The null footage is short: 12.6\,s for Ped2 (three pre-onset
segments), 6.9\,min for Avenue (16 videos) and 26.9\,min for Mivia (50 negative
videos). At this length the counts are too small to resolve the nominal level. Ped2 flow has \RThreePedTwoFlowFAk{}/\RThreePedTwoFlowFAn{} false
alarms, Ped2 PatchCore has \RThreePedTwoPCFAk{}/\RThreePedTwoPCFAn{}, Avenue
flow has \RThreeAvenueFlowFAk{}/\RThreeAvenueFlowFAn{} and Avenue PatchCore has
\RThreeAvenuePCFAk{}/\RThreeAvenuePCFAn{}. The intervals are wide. Ped2 flow at
$\alpha=0.2$ has 3 of 3 false alarms, with selected lag 1 (Online Resource~1, Table~S1),
so the Ped2 count is sensitive to the level chosen. Mivia flow has
\RThreeMiviaFlowFAk{}/\RThreeMiviaFlowFAn{} false alarms, inside the level.
Mivia PatchCore has \RThreeMiviaPCFAk{}/\RThreeMiviaPCFAn{}, a proportion of
\RThreeMiviaPCFAp{} with interval $[\RThreeMiviaPCFALo{},\RThreeMiviaPCFAHi{}]$.
Mivia with the supervised backbone has \RThreeMiviaSupFAk{}/\RThreeMiviaSupFAn{}, a
proportion of \RThreeMiviaSupFAp{} with interval
$[\RThreeMiviaSupFALo{},\RThreeMiviaSupFAHi{}]$. The PatchCore interval contains $0.1$.
The one-sided exact binomial probability of at least nine false alarms in 50 videos
at $p=0.1$ is $0.058$. The PatchCore count also moves with $\alpha$: it is 3 of 50
at $\alpha=0.05$ and 12 of 50 at $\alpha=0.2$ (full sweep in Online Resource~1,
Sect.~S1). The supervised count is 0 of 50 at $\alpha=0.05$ and 7 of 50 at
$\alpha=0.2$. Several candidate explanations fit the design, and 50 null videos
cannot separate them. First, Mivia calibrates across cameras, but the guarantee is
per camera, and every test camera is unseen, so the per-camera premise is not tested
here. Second, the p-values are serially dependent (the paragraph below). Third, the
lag and the alarm boundary are chosen on training videos. The selected lag is the
smallest one whose LOVO proportion is at most $\alpha$. For Mivia PatchCore,
$L^\ast=7$ has 20 of 200 $=0.100$, exactly at $\alpha$, and selection at a boundary
can pick a lag whose true rate lies above $\alpha$.

Per-video rates understate what an operator sees, because the detector restarts after
each alarm. Per hour of null footage, SENTINEL-E raises 284.8 false alarms on Ped2
flow, from one alarm in 12.6\,s. On Avenue PatchCore it raises 86.6 per hour, and all
ten of its alarms come from one null video in 6.9\,min. On Mivia PatchCore it raises
100.5 per hour, with 45 alarms across nine videos in 26.9\,min. With the supervised
backbone it raises \RThreeMiviaSupFAhrP{} per hour on the same negative footage. A
per-video proportion counts a video once, however many alarms it carries. The hourly
figures rest on very short null footage and are illustrations, not operating rates.

With PatchCore, per-camera warm-up normalisation ($W=\RFourWarmPCW{}$\,s, selected on training videos) lowers the per-video false alarms at $\alpha=0.1$ from \RFourWarmPCRawFAk{}/\RFourWarmPCRawFAn{} to \RFourWarmPCNormFAk{}/\RFourWarmPCNormFAn{}, at a detection cost of \RFourWarmPCNormDetk{}/\RFourWarmPCNormDetn{} against \RFourWarmPCRawDetk{}/\RFourWarmPCRawDetn{} episodes (Online Resource~1, Sect.~S1).

The second finding concerns power, which depends strongly on the backbone.
Ped2 flow detects \RThreePedTwoFlowDetk{} of \RThreePedTwoFlowDetn{} episodes
and Ped2 PatchCore detects \RThreePedTwoPCDetk{} of \RThreePedTwoPCDetn{}.
Avenue detects only \RThreeAvenueFlowDetk{} of \RThreeAvenueFlowDetn{} with
flow and \RThreeAvenuePCDetk{} of \RThreeAvenuePCDetn{} with PatchCore. Mivia
flow detects none of its \RThreeMiviaFlowDetn{} positive videos. Mivia
PatchCore detects \RThreeMiviaPCDetk{} of \RThreeMiviaPCDetn{}, with a median delay of
\RThreeMiviaPCDelayMed{}\,s after onset. The supervised backbone detects
\RThreeMiviaSupDetk{} of \RThreeMiviaSupDetn{}, with a median delay of
\RThreeMiviaSupDelayMed{}\,s. PatchCore detects more episodes than flow on Avenue and
Mivia-IWDD, and flow detects more on Ped2.

The third finding concerns the baselines. On Mivia PatchCore every baseline also has a per-video false-alarm proportion above $0.1$ on test, ranging from 9 to 18 of 50 negative videos. With the supervised backbone the picture is different. Among the methods whose test per-video proportion is at most $\alpha$ in point estimate (every interval contains $0.1$) (SENTINEL-E \RThreeSupSEFAk{}/50, p-value threshold \RThreeSupPValueFAk{}/50, fixed threshold \RThreeSupFixedFAk{}/50, CUSUM \RThreeSupCUSUMFAk{}/50), SENTINEL-E detects the most episodes, \RThreeSupSEDetk{} of 50, tied with the p-value threshold. The p-value threshold raises \RThreeSupPValueFAhrP{} false alarms per hour of normal footage, against \RThreeSupSEFAhrP{} for SENTINEL-E. The two methods that detect more, the parametric e-detector (\RThreeSupParamDetk{} of 50 episodes) and E-SHIFT (\RThreeSupEShiftDetk{} of 50), have \RThreeSupParamFAk{} and \RThreeSupEShiftFAk{} false-alarm videos in 50, point estimates above the nominal level. On Avenue PatchCore, three of the six baselines (CUSUM, Shiryaev--Roberts and the parametric e-detector) had no admissible knob at $\alpha=0.1$ and used the most conservative setting. Across all \RThreeBaselineConfigs{} baseline configurations at this level, \RThreeBaselineNoKnob{} had none. The fixed threshold and E-SHIFT detect more episodes than SENTINEL-E on Avenue PatchCore (15 and 29 of 74, against 9), with 2 and 4 false alarms in 16 videos. On Mivia PatchCore, CUSUM matches the 9 false alarms in 50 videos, detects more episodes (11 of 50 against 10) and has a lower rate per hour (53.6 against 100.5). Its knob is the most conservative in its sweep, so this is not a tuned baseline. On Ped2 PatchCore the fixed threshold detects 8 of 12 episodes against 7 of 12, with no false alarm in three null videos, which is too little evidence to rule out an excess. On these short videos the baselines at their selected knobs typically detect more episodes than SENTINEL-E, generally with more false alarms. With the supervised Mivia-IWDD backbone the comparison changes, as described next. Its claimed advantage is the horizon-free guarantee, which the short real videos cannot exercise and the simulations in \cref{sec:validity} test.

\paragraph{Dependence.} The guarantee rests on the thinned p-values, so
\cref{tab:r3_dependence} measures their dependence. Two quantities are
reported. The pooled autocorrelation of the concatenated training scores
exceeds $0.05$ at every lag up to 200. That length reflects differences
between the mean scores of videos, not the time scale of the process, and
it is not used. The within-video autocorrelation is the relevant quantity. At
lag one it averages \RThreeWithinLagOneMin{} to \RThreeWithinLagOneMax{}, and for the
supervised Mivia backbone \RThreeMiviaSupWithinLagOne{}. At lag 25 it averages between
essentially zero and \RThreeWithinLagTwentyFiveMax{}, and for the supervised backbone
\RThreeMiviaSupWithinLagTwentyFive{}. Lag 25 is 1\,s at 25\,fps and 2.5\,s at 10\,fps.

The thinned p-values remain positively correlated. At the selected lags the
lag-one correlation ranges from \RThreeDepCorrMin{} to \RThreeDepCorrMax{}, and for the
supervised backbone it is \RThreeMiviaSupDepCorr{}. The empirical CDF shows no excess
of small values. The largest positive deviation is \RThreeDepSupMax{} at the selected
lags, and \RThreeDepSupOneMax{} at lag one.
The conditional-mean diagnostic departs from conditional super-uniformity. If $p_t$
is conditionally super-uniform given the past, its mean given $p_{t-1}\le0.1$ is at
least $0.5$. The observed means lie between \RThreeDepCondMin{} and
\RThreeDepCondMax{}, on \RThreeDepCountMin{} to \RThreeDepCountMax{} pairs; for the
supervised backbone the mean is \RThreeMiviaSupDepCond{}. A small
p-value is followed by another small one. The hypothesis of \cref{thm:episodic} is
therefore not met by these thinned streams, so the real-video results are evidence
about the per-video rate, not a check of the theorem's conditions. The observable
consequence is the per-video false-alarm rate. On Ped2 and Avenue it is consistent
with $0.1$, but the null footage is short. On Mivia PatchCore the point estimate is
$\RThreeMiviaPCFAp{}$, and its interval contains $0.1$. On Mivia with the supervised
backbone the point estimate is $\RThreeMiviaSupFAp{}$, and its interval also contains $0.1$.

\paragraph{Action or residue.} The Mivia labels mark only the onset. They do
not say whether the detector responds to the dumping action or to the waste
left behind. Flow measures motion. Flow scores are higher on negative videos
than before onset in positive videos, so flow can at best respond to the
action, and the motion of other activity in the scene confuses it. Appearance
can in principle see deposited waste. Under PatchCore, static dumping is
detected in \RThreeMiviaPCStaticDetk{} of \RThreeMiviaPCStaticDetn{} videos and
dynamic dumping in \RThreeMiviaPCDynDetk{} of \RThreeMiviaPCDynDetn{}. These
counts do not differ at this sample size. With the supervised backbone the counts are
\RThreeMiviaSupStaticDetk{} of \RThreeMiviaSupStaticDetn{} static and
\RThreeMiviaSupDynDetk{} of \RThreeMiviaSupDynDetn{} dynamic, again not different at this
size. A supervised detector may learn either cue, and the labels do not show which
one it uses. The per-type split for all methods is in Online Resource 1, Sect.~S1. A
residue that persists after the action is the permanent-change case, in which the
episode never ends, $\eta=0$. The episodic mixture contains that case. A dedicated
residue model would change the background model itself, and we leave it to future
work.

\paragraph{Scope.} The real test videos last seconds to about a minute. The real data therefore bear only weakly on per-video false-alarm control, because
the null footage is short. The long-horizon
claim rests on the simulated streams in \cref{sec:validity}, which run for up
to a week of simulated time.
