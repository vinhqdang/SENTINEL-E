\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Power on real video depends on the backbone.}
Optical flow detects 11 of 12 Ped2 episodes, but 3 of 74 Avenue episodes and none of the 50 Mivia-IWDD dumping videos. PatchCore detects 7, 9 and 10 respectively (\cref{tab:r3_test}). PatchCore is a strong training-free appearance backbone, but it is not the state of the art on these benchmarks (\cref{sec:real-data}). The power figures therefore bound what such frozen backbones deliver, not what the best published detectors would deliver. On Mivia-IWDD, PatchCore detects 5 of 25 static and 5 of 25 dynamic dumping events, and flow detects none (Online Resource~1, Table~S3). The labels give onset only, so this split cannot show whether the detector responds to the action or to the waste left behind. A persistent residue is the permanent-change special case of the episodic mixture, but no run here tests an appearance backbone on residue.

\paragraph{False alarms on real video are consistent with the nominal level, but the evidence is thin.}
On the single-camera corpora the false-alarm counts are 1 of 3 and 0 of 3 for Ped2, and 0 of 16 and 1 of 16 for Avenue. The null footage is 12.6\,s of Ped2 and 6.9\,min of Avenue, too little to resolve per-video rates. Ped2 flow at $\alpha=0.2$ has 3 of 3 false alarms (Online Resource~1, Table~S1). On Mivia-IWDD at $\alpha=0.1$, PatchCore raises alarms on 9 of 50 normal videos, with a 95\% interval of [0.086, 0.314] that contains the nominal level, and flow raises them on 4 of 50. Per hour of null footage the rates are far higher than the per-video figures suggest, because the detector restarts after each alarm and alarms cluster within a video (\cref{sec:real-data}).

\paragraph{Candidate causes of the Mivia-IWDD excess.}
The point estimate exceeds the nominal level, and the data do not say why. Three candidates fit the design. Pooled calibration across cameras is the first. The guarantee is per camera, and every test camera is unseen, so the per-camera premise is not tested. Serial dependence of the p-values is the second (next paragraph). Boundary selection of the lag is the third. $L^\ast=7$ is the smallest lag whose leave-one-video-out proportion is at most $\alpha$, and it sits exactly on the boundary at 20 of 200 $=0.100$. Fifty null videos cannot separate these effects, and this paper names none of them as the cause.

\paragraph{Real-video baselines show no demonstrated advantage.}
On the real-video comparisons in \cref{sec:real-data}, SENTINEL-E shows no advantage over the baselines. On Mivia-IWDD, PatchCore CUSUM has the same per-video false-alarm count (9 of 50), detects more episodes (11 against 10) and has a lower rate per hour (53.6 against 100.5). Fifteen of the 36 baseline configurations at $\alpha=0.1$ had no admissible knob and used the most conservative setting, so those baselines are not tuned. The advantage claimed for SENTINEL-E is the horizon-free guarantee, and the short real videos cannot exercise it.

\paragraph{Conditional super-uniformity does not hold on real video.}
\Cref{thm:episodic} requires the betting p-values to be super-uniform given the past. Thinning does not deliver this on real footage, so the hypothesis fails there. The thinned p-values remain positively correlated (lag-one correlation between \RThreeDepCorrMin{} and \RThreeDepCorrMax{} at the selected lags), and after a p-value below $0.1$ the next has mean \RThreeDepCondMin{} to \RThreeDepCondMax{}, far below the $0.5$ that conditional super-uniformity implies (\cref{tab:r3_dependence}). The Ljung--Box test also rejects independence for most simulated cameras \citep{ljung1978}. The marginal distribution shows no excess of small values, and the per-video false-alarm rate is consistent with $0.1$ on Ped2 and Avenue, where the null footage is short. Neither repairs the formal argument. On real video the time-uniform bound is therefore an empirical property of the selected operating point, not a theorem. Betting on blocks rather than frames, or e-processes built for dependent data, would be needed to restore it formally.

\paragraph{Long-horizon evidence is simulated.}
The real videos last seconds to minutes, so the week-long result rests on simulation. \Cref{tab:horizon_week} runs 200 streams per horizon from 1\,h to 168\,h, with a simulated 24\,h diurnal cycle at 25\,fps. SENTINEL-E raised no false alarm at any horizon. Its 95\% upper limit is 0.019, which does not by itself resolve $\alpha=0.01$. The fixed threshold reaches 0.980 within an hour. The simulator's assumptions, namely the diurnal and weather processes and Student-$t$ AR(1) noise, are untested against weeks of real footage. A slow drift invisible within a day could compound over a week, and no experiment here shows either way. The time-uniform guarantee does not depend on the horizon, so this is a question about the simulator, not the theorem. The shorter sweep, up to \HorizonMaxHours\,h, is in Online Resource~1, Table~S6.

\paragraph{Covariate shift is the main threat to calibration.}
The unweighted residual default holds its level at $\times 1.0$ and $\times 1.5$, but at $\times 3.0$ its false-alarm probability is 0.0620 (\cref{tab:shift_remedy}). A stricter support region, with slack 0.5, holds the level at every shift tested, at the cost of abstention. Its selection is disclosed in \cref{sec:shift}. Seven pilot variants were tried, only two met the pre-set criterion, and the evidence covers one activity-shift family in one direction. Likelihood-ratio weighting also holds the level, but its miss rate reaches 1.000 at $\times 3.0$, and its effective-sample-size substitution has no proof. The context gate fired on every null stream, so it is not a usable monitor. Operators should watch the abstention rate. As a suggestion, not a validated rule, recalibrate when the abstention fraction exceeds 0.05. A miss rate that rises under an unmodelled shift produces fewer alarms, not more, so the alarm stream alone does not reveal it.

\paragraph{The learned spatial prior does not improve delay.}
The learned prior keeps the false-alarm guarantee, as \cref{thm:predictable} requires, but it does not shorten delay (\cref{sec:network}). Its transfer to other camera topologies is untested.

\paragraph{The episodic advantage is configuration-specific.}
In the corner sweep (\cref{tab:corner_sweep}), episodes help when they are long, intermittent and recurring. With 1600-frame episodes, $\pi=0.2$ and a 12{,}000-frame gap, the episodic mixture is ahead by 141.1\,s (95\% CI +79.6 to +210.2\,s). A single 800-frame episode favours the changepoint mixture by 55.7\,s (95\% CI $-111.2$ to $-14.0$). Dense and permanent changes are equivalent under the 1\,s margin of \cref{sec:episodes}, and 200-frame episodes are missed by both mixtures. The delay comparison and the ablation use one configuration, four episodes in 60 minutes (\cref{sec:delay}). Unpaired comparisons on different grids are descriptive (Online Resource~1, Tables~S4 and~S5). The guarantee does not depend on the episode model, but power does, and heavy-tailed episode durations, which dumping may produce, are untested.

\paragraph{Deployment needs more than a false-alarm rate.}
The time-uniform guarantee is the horizon-free analogue of the in-control run length $\ARL$. Control charts also need a recalibration cadence, which statistical process control treats as a design choice \citep{montgomery2020introduction}, and this paper does not set one. A ROC operating point \citep{fawcett2006introduction} describes a per-decision trade-off that control rooms already use for other sensors. By contrast, $\alpha$ bounds false alarms over a whole monitoring period, so an alarm budget per period is the operational counterpart. Hourly alarm rates on real video are large (\cref{sec:real-data}), and a deployment should budget them as well as per-video rates. Choosing $\alpha$ against the costs of a false dispatch and a missed event is left open. Every alarm should go to a named human reviewer before any action. Public-space surveillance also raises accountability questions that the guarantee does not answer. A deployment needs a retention policy, a named reviewer before any enforcement step, and a route by which residents can contest a flagged event.
